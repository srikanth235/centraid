Chapter 3 · devices

The phone is the vault. Your machines keep it safe.

Centraid refuses the hardest problem in personal software — multi-master sync — by never having it. Your phone holds the vault: the one copy anything writes to, and the only device that holds its keys. Gateways — any machine you control running centraid-gateway, the laptop at home, a VPS, a NAS — keep sealed copies they cannot open, reached straight over HTTPS with no relay service to subscribe to. This chapter is that arrangement, wire by wire.

§01 · topology

One writer, never a mesh.

The Rust core on the phone is the vault's sole authority and writer — apps, the camera roll and your own taps all go through it, and nothing else ever touches the database file. That single fact buys the consent story (one door to check), the audit story (one place receipts mint) and the sync story (there is nothing to reconcile, because there is one writer). A gateway is not a second home for the vault; it is where the vault's backup lives:

the vault

Your phone

The native app over the Rust core holds vault.db and the keys your 24 words derive. It works with no network at all; backing up is what it does when a gateway is in reach.

the backup

A blind store

centraid-gateway serve on a laptop, a spare machine or a VPS. It keeps sealed objects and a snapshot history per vault — no key, no plaintext, no schema — and a phone may back up to several.

The trade is explicit: the phone becomes the thing to protect — which is why its backup is sealed snapshots on machines you choose, and why 24 words bring it back onto a new phone.

§02 · addressing

Every request names its vault.

A gateway can keep the backups of several vaults — yours, a household member's — so no request is ever "to the gateway": every route lives under /v2/v/{vault}, where a vault's id is its identity public key, and the bearer token a request carries was minted for that vault alone. A token for one vault opens nothing of another's, and an unknown vault gets the same refusal as a foreign token, so neither is an oracle.

who may write

One writer per vault, by epoch

Each vault has a writer epoch on every gateway, and each token carries the epoch it was minted at. A write under an older one is refused MOVED: when a vault moves to a new phone, the old one stops at its next upload.

two planes

The operator holds ciphertext

Pairing QRs, health checks and scrubs are the gateway's own commands (centraid-gateway pair / pairings / health / scrub), run by whoever holds the machine. Everything they touch was sealed on the phone first, so even the gateway's operator holds nothing that opens.

§03 · pairing

A QR that admits one vault, once.

Adding a gateway is a one-time QR: centraid-gateway serve prints one while nothing has paired, and centraid-gateway pair prints a fresh one beside a running serve. It carries the gateway's id, the addresses to dial, the pin — the BLAKE3 hash of the gateway's certificate — and a secret that admits one vault, once, within 24 hours. The gateway keeps only the secret's hash.

The phone — Pair with your laptop on the More sheet, or Add a gateway on the Backup screen — scans it, dials the addresses in order, refuses any certificate whose hash is not the pin, and trades the secret for a bearer token for its vault. No password, no account, nothing reusable to intercept: a spent, an expired and an unknown secret all get the same refusal.

Then you compare a safety number — 60 digits in 12 groups of 5, drawn by one function on both screens from the vault's identity key and the certificate's pin. A phone may pair with more than one gateway from the first day, each with its own QR, and backs up to whichever it can reach; a second gateway somewhere else is your off-site copy.

pairing · the gateway's shell
$ centraid-gateway pair --data-dir ~/centraid-gateway
{ "v": 2, "gw": "…", "addrs": ["192.168.1.20:8443", "laptop.local:8443"], "pin": "…", "secret": "…", "exp_ms": … } # and the same text as a QR
$ centraid-gateway pairings --data-dir ~/centraid-gateway
# each vault: its writer epoch, its head, its tokens, its safety number

§04 · connectivity

HTTPS to a machine you own, no rented middleman.

Phone and gateway speak plain HTTPS, straight from one to the other. The gateway mints its own certificate — ECDSA P-256, because Apple's TLS stack refuses an Ed25519 one — and no certificate authority vouches for it: the phone trusts exactly the certificate whose hash the pairing QR named. No relay, no push service, no sync fleet, no domain to buy.

1

LAN first

At home the phone finds its gateway on the local network — serve advertises itself over Bonjour — and keeps the last addresses that answered, so an upload the system runs in the background needs no lookup. Away from home a gateway works at any address the phone can dial directly: a VPS, a name on your own VPN. Nothing punches holes through NATs and nothing relays; a gateway the phone cannot reach is one it waits for.

2

HTTPS because of the iPhone

An iPhone keeps uploading for a suspended app only through the system's background upload session, and that session speaks HTTPS. So the phone seals what it backs up into a bounded spool and hands the files to the system, which uploads them while the app sleeps — under the cellular allowance you set — and wakes it to record each acknowledgement. Android moves them in its own background windows.

3

One version, in the path

Every route lives under /v2 and a pairing QR carries v: 2; a phone refuses a QR it cannot read, and a change that breaks a route is /v3. No negotiation, no fallback — and every refusal is a typed code the phone turns into its own sentence, never a bare status it has to guess at.

A proxy that terminates TLS in front of a gateway presents its own certificate, and the phone refuses it; a port forward or a TCP pass-through is fine.

§05 · one writer

Offline, without a second writer.

The vault is on the phone, so offline is the normal case rather than a degraded one: every read and write runs against the vault in your pocket, and the network matters only to the backup, which catches up when a gateway is in reach. There is no replica to keep current, no second writer and no two-way merge.

What the gateways keep is snapshots: the vault copied page for page, cut into small ranges sealed under names derived from their own bytes, so an hour of edits uploads only the ranges that changed. "Backed up" means a gateway acknowledged it, and no screen says more than that.

1

A restore reads before it claims

Restoring onto a new phone starts from your 24 words and a read-only grant: the phone fetches the newest snapshot and checks every range, the file's hash and its row counts against the manifest before it trusts a byte of it.

2

Then it claims the vault

Only a checked snapshot earns a claim: the next writer epoch, signed by the vault's identity key and naming the head it checked. A head that moved in the meantime moves no writer until the new one has been checked too.

3

The old phone freezes, it does not fork

The phone that lost the claim keeps what it has, readable, and stops writing: its next upload is refused MOVED, and it says so rather than carry on as a second master.

This is how one vault survives a lost phone without ever growing a second master: the gateways hold sealed history, and the writer epoch decides which phone may add to it.

§06 · desktop shell

Not in v0. The laptop is a gateway.

There is no desktop shell in this release: v0 ships the phone app and the gateway, and nothing but the phone holds the vault. A laptop's part is to keep the backup — centraid-gateway serve, holding sealed copies of your phone's vault — and centraid-gateway install brings it back after a reboot.

install writes a systemd user unit or a macOS LaunchAgent and prints the command that starts it — it never enables what it writes, because a service that starts because a file was unpacked is one nobody chose to run.

§07 · web app

No browser door in v0.

There is no web app in this release, and a gateway could not serve one: it holds no key and no plaintext, only sealed objects for the phone that sealed them. The vault is read where it lives — on the phone, in the native app.

§08 · the phone app

Native on both platforms, and the vault itself.

The phone app is native on iOS and Android — SwiftUI and Jetpack Compose views over one Kotlin Multiplatform module and the Rust core, linked through a C interface. The core holds the vault; the views draw what it answers. Every first-party app is a native screen, and everything works in airplane mode, because the vault every screen reads is on the phone.

The backup runs on its own. Each pass prepares — a snapshot when one is due, then everything no gateway has confirmed, sealed into a bounded spool — moves it to a reachable gateway, and settles what was acknowledged. Your transfer rule decides what may cross a metered link: by default no original does, and a video's original never does; thumbnails and previews always go first, because they are the grid.

Two touches earn a mention. Backed up means a gateway acknowledged it — the Backup screen and the line on Home never claim more. And Free up space offers only originals a gateway has acknowledged and no kept album holds, then hands them to the system's own delete confirmation; the photo stays in Centraid's grid with its thumbnail, and its original comes back from a gateway when you open it.

Pre-release note: the apps build from source, from the repository's mobile/ directory.

§09 · harness runtimes

No brain on the gateway.

There are no agent runtimes in this release. A gateway runs nothing but its blind store — it holds no key and no plaintext, so there is nothing on it for an agent to read — and the assistant and automation planes are not part of v0.
